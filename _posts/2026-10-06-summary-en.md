## [Francis Halzen Wins 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for IceCube](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2026/) ⭐️ 9.0/10

Francis Halzen of the University of Wisconsin–Madison has been awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics for conceiving the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, a cubic-kilometer detector buried deep in the Antarctic ice at the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station. This recognition validates decades of work in neutrino astronomy, a field that opens a fundamentally new observational window into the cosmos by detecting particles that travel through matter almost unimpeded. IceCube&\#x27;s detection of high-energy neutrinos from astrophysical sources marks a milestone in multi-messenger astronomy, complementing observations made with light and gravitational waves. IceCube detects neutrinos indirectly: when a neutrino occasionally interacts with the Antarctic ice, it produces a charged particle that travels faster than the speed of light in the ice medium, generating Cherenkov radiation that is captured by the array of optical sensors buried to a depth of about 2,500 meters.

hackernews · Hacker News \(热门\) · Oct 6, 09:48 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49976265)

**Background**: Neutrinos are elementary subatomic particles with zero electric charge and near-zero mass that interact only via the weak nuclear force and gravity, earning them the nickname &\#x27;ghost particles.&\#x27; They are produced abundantly by nuclear reactions in stars, supernovae, and radioactive decay, and trillions pass through a planet without interaction. The IceCube observatory, developed by the University of Wisconsin–Madison and recognized as a CERN experiment \(RE10\), was built to capture the rare interactions of these elusive particles by instrumenting a massive volume of natural Antarctic ice.

## [OpenTPU: AI-Designed Open-Source AI Accelerator](https://github.com/FeSens/openTPU) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A new open-source AI inference accelerator called OpenTPU has been released on GitHub, with its architecture designed entirely by AI using recursive self-improvement. The project can now run modern models such as Qwen and Gemma at over 80 tokens per second, having evolved from only a few tokens per second at the start. This project represents one of the first tangible demonstrations of AI designing its own hardware, blurring the line between AI-generated software and AI-generated silicon. It signals a potential future where AI systems can autonomously co-design the chips they run on, with major implications for hardware democratization, chip design economics, and the feasibility of recursive self-improvement. The same AI-driven technique previously used to develop open-source RISC-V CPU cores was applied to create OpenTPU, and performance improved from a few tokens/sec to 80+ tokens/sec through iterative self-improvement loops. The design specifically targets inference workloads rather than training, and currently runs smaller variants of frontier models at high throughput.

**Background**: Tensor Processing Units \(TPUs\) are application-specific integrated circuits \(ASICs\) originally designed by Google to accelerate machine learning workloads, particularly matrix multiplications central to neural network inference and training. Recursive self-improvement \(RSI\) refers to a theoretical process where an AI system rewrites and tests its own code, progressively enhancing its own capabilities. This project sits at the intersection of these two concepts: an AI system improving its own hardware substrate. Open-source RISC-V CPU cores have previously been a proving ground for AI-driven hardware design, and OpenTPU extends that lineage to AI accelerators.

## [Paper Claims Subquadratic 3SUM and Subcubic APSP Algorithms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06783) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A newly posted arXiv paper claims to provide subquadratic-time algorithms for the 3SUM problem and subcubic-time algorithms for All-Pairs Shortest Path \(APSP\), results that, if correct, would break long-standing conjectures in fine-grained complexity theory. The paper is currently on Hacker News&\#x27;s front page, drawing significant attention from the algorithms and complexity theory communities. If validated, this result would constitute a major theoretical breakthrough, since the 3SUM conjecture and APSP conjecture are foundational pillars supporting a wide web of conditional lower bounds across combinatorial and graph problems. A positive resolution would force re-examination of dozens of algorithmic hardness results that currently rely on these conjectures. Subquadratic 3SUM and subcubic APSP have resisted decades of attempts; the paper&\#x27;s claim, if true, would imply collapse-style consequences across fine-grained complexity. The result has not yet been peer-reviewed or independently verified, so the community is treating it with cautious skepticism pending scrutiny of the proofs.

rss · Hacker News \(热门\) · Oct 6, 12:31

**Background**: The 3SUM problem asks whether, given a set of numbers, three of them can be found whose sum equals zero, with the textbook solution running in roughly quadratic time. APSP asks for the shortest-path distance between every pair of vertices in a weighted graph, with classical algorithms like Floyd–Warshall running in cubic time. Fine-grained complexity theory posits strong conjectures—that 3SUM requires quadratic time and APSP requires cubic time in certain settings—which are used as the basis for conditional lower bounds on many other algorithmic problems.

## [Dust: Pretraining Transformers Without Backpropagation](https://qlabs.sh/research/dust) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Researchers have introduced Dust, a method that pretrains transformers using zeroth-order gradient estimation instead of standard backpropagation, enabling parameter updates derived only from function-value queries rather than explicit gradient computation. The approach challenges the long-standing assumption that backpropagation is indispensable for training large neural networks. If zeroth-order methods can scale to transformer pretraining at competitive efficiency, it could reshape assumptions about what hardware and software constraints are fundamental to deep learning, and open doors in settings where backpropagation is infeasible. The proposal is also significant because it revisits a recurring question in optimization research about whether gradient-free methods can ever rival first-order methods at scale. Zeroth-order optimization approximates gradients by perturbing parameters and observing changes in the loss, rather than computing exact derivatives via the chain rule. The method has theoretical convergence guarantees for smooth objectives but typically requires many more function evaluations per step than backpropagation, raising serious questions about its practical compute efficiency for large-scale pretraining.

**Background**: Backpropagation has been the de facto standard for training neural networks since the 1980s because it efficiently computes exact gradients of the loss with respect to every parameter using the chain rule. Zeroth-order optimization is a derivative-free alternative that estimates gradients by sampling function values along random directions, traditionally used in black-box settings such as hyperparameter tuning or problems with discontinuous, noisy, or non-differentiable objectives where gradients are unavailable or unreliable. The central question surrounding Dust is whether this older paradigm can be made competitive with backpropagation for modern, large-scale transformer pretraining.
