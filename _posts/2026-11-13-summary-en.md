A developer reverse-engineered a Coway Airmega IconS air purifier and replaced its reliance on Coway&\#x27;s cloud service with local control, including integration through Home Assistant. The project exposed security and engineering weaknesses, notably that the fan downloads its root certificate authority certificate over plain HTTP. Local control can preserve functionality when cloud services are unreliable and reduce privacy concerns associated with routing appliance data through vendor-operated servers. The work also demonstrates how reverse engineering can reveal practical IoT security problems and inspire broader efforts to liberate connected appliances from unnecessary cloud dependencies. The researcher used a man-in-the-middle Raspberry Pi access point running mitmproxy to intercept traffic between the purifier and the Coway IoCare cloud, then substituted his own certificate to analyze the protocol. The purifier&\#x27;s use of plain HTTP for certificate delivery made interception possible and highlighted avoidable weaknesses in the appliance&\#x27;s trust and update architecture.

**Background**: Connected appliances often rely on vendor clouds for commands, status reporting, firmware delivery, and integration with smart-home platforms. A “de-clouding” project seeks to recover those functions locally by reverse-engineering the device communication protocol and bridging it to systems such as Home Assistant. This approach can improve availability and autonomy, but it may also depend on unofficial interfaces and expose the device to additional security risks.

AI-assisted manuscript decoding builds on a broader effort to use machine learning for historical handwriting and formal mathematics, but transcription alone does not prove that every recovered passage is correct.
