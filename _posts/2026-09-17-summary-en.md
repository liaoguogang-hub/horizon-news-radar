## [Levetiracetam Repurposed as Therapy for Diffuse Midline Glioma](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04646-6) ⭐️ 8.0/10

A study published in Nature Medicine on September 17, 2026, reports that the antiseizure drug levetiracetam extends survival and reduces tumor growth in diffuse midline glioma \(DMG\) by selectively dampening GABAergic synaptic signaling through a mechanism independent of its known SV2A target. DMG is a fatal pediatric brain tumor with a median survival of only 9–15 months and no effective chemotherapy options. Because levetiracetam is already widely available, inexpensive, and has a well-characterized safety profile, this drug-repurposing finding could rapidly translate into clinical trials and potentially improve outcomes for children facing this devastating disease. The therapeutic effect was specific to DMG and was not observed in hemispheric high-grade gliomas, suggesting tumor-type selectivity. The mechanism bypasses levetiracetam&\#x27;s canonical SV2A-mediated antiseizure pathway, pointing to a non-canonical GABAergic synapse-targeted mode of action that could be exploited independently of seizure control.

rss · Nature Medicine · Sep 17, 00:00

**Background**: Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered \(DMG\), is a WHO grade 4 brain tumor that arises in midline structures such as the brainstem, thalamus, and spinal cord; when located in the pons it is known as DIPG. It primarily affects children \(median age 6–7\) and has a median survival of only 9–15 months, as its location precludes surgical resection and chemotherapy has proven ineffective. GABAergic synapses are inhibitory neuronal connections that use the neurotransmitter GABA; SV2A \(synaptic vesicle protein 2A\) is the established binding target through which levetiracetam exerts its antiseizure effects, but this new work identifies an SV2A-independent pathway operating in DMG tumors.

## [Bonsai 2 27B: Near-Lossless Compression in 9x Smaller Footprint](https://prismml.com/news/bonsai-2-27b) ⭐️ 7.0/10

PrismML has released Bonsai 2 27B, a compressed language model that retains 98.2% of Qwen3.8 27B&\#x27;s benchmark performance while fitting into a 5.9GB footprint — roughly 9x smaller than typical 27B-class models — and supports multimodal and agentic capabilities. This release demonstrates that 27B-class multimodal models can be compressed to run efficiently on local devices without major quality loss, potentially lowering hardware barriers for deploying capable LLMs on consumer hardware and edge devices. Bonsai 2 27B is described as a &\#x27;Ternary&\#x27; model, suggesting it uses ternary \(3-level\) weight quantization as part of its compression pipeline; it is available on Hugging Face as a GGUF file compatible with tools like LM Studio and Docker model runners.

rss · Hacker News \(热门\) · Sep 17, 21:13

**Background**: Large language models in the 27B parameter range typically require tens of gigabytes of memory at standard precision \(e.g., FP16\), making them impractical for many local deployment scenarios. Model compression techniques such as quantization \(reducing numerical precision of weights\) and pruning \(removing redundant weights\) can shrink models substantially, but often at the cost of output quality. Ternary quantization is an aggressive form that constrains weights to roughly three values, achieving very high compression ratios. Near-lossless compression means the compressed model retains nearly all of the original&\#x27;s measured performance on standard benchmarks.

While the original blog excerpt provides limited technical detail, Phoronix&\#x27;s coverage indicates the attacks specifically target key Rust language developers and owners of widely-used crates, suggesting sophisticated social engineering rather than opportunistic phishing.

Because the Rust ecosystem relies heavily on third-party crates, compromise of a single popular maintainer account can cascade into many downstream projects, making supply chain integrity a critical concern. Targeted social engineering attacks on developers have become increasingly common, as attackers exploit publicly available information about contributors, their projects, and their collaborators to craft convincing lures.
