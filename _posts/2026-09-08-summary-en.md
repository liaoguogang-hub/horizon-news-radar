## [Phase 3 Trial Finds No Survival Benefit Adding Atezolizumab to SBRT in Early-Stage NSCLC](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2826%2901655-7/fulltext?rss=yes) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The SWOG/NRG S1914 phase 3 randomized controlled trial reported that adding induction and consolidation atezolizumab to stereotactic body radiation therapy \(SBRT\) did not improve overall survival compared with SBRT alone in patients with high-risk, early-stage non-small-cell lung cancer \(NSCLC\), and was associated with more grade 3 or higher adverse events. This is the first fully reported phase 3 cooperative group trial to evaluate immunotherapy in inoperable early-stage NSCLC, and its negative result will shape clinical guidelines and redirect research efforts away from this particular combination strategy for patients who cannot undergo surgery. The trial used atezolizumab, a PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitor, in an induction, concurrent, and adjuvant schedule around SBRT. The combination increased grade 3 or higher toxicity without delivering a survival advantage in this high-risk early-stage population.

rss · The Lancet · 最新文章 · Sep 6, 22:30

**Background**: Non-small-cell lung cancer \(NSCLC\) accounts for the majority of lung cancer cases. Patients with early-stage disease are typically treated with surgery, but those who are medically inoperable often receive stereotactic body radiation therapy \(SBRT\), a precise form of high-dose radiation delivered in one to five sessions. Atezolizumab is an immune checkpoint inhibitor that blocks PD-L1, a protein some cancer cells use to evade immune attack, and it has established benefits in advanced and locally advanced NSCLC. The SWOG/NRG S1914 trial tested whether adding this immunotherapy around SBRT could improve outcomes in patients with early-stage but high-risk disease who cannot undergo surgery.

## [C\*: Unifying Programming and Formal Verification in C](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.02246) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A new research paper introduces C\*, a system designed to unify programming and formal verification within the C language. The work aims to make formal verification more accessible to C developers by integrating it directly into the programming workflow rather than treating it as a separate, post-hoc process. C remains one of the most widely used systems programming languages, yet formal verification tooling for it is relatively scarce and difficult to use. If C\* successfully lowers the barrier to verification, it could significantly improve the reliability and security of safety-critical systems software written in C, such as operating system kernels and embedded code. The paper appears on arXiv \(2504.02246\), positioning C\* as a unification layer between writing C code and proving its correctness. Readers seeking specifics would need to consult the full paper for details on the verification logic used, annotation burden, and performance characteristics.

rss · Hacker News \(热门\) · Sep 8, 15:56

**Background**: Formal verification is the mathematical proof of a system&\#x27;s correctness against a formal specification, and it underpins high-assurance software such as the CompCert verified C compiler and the seL4 microkernel. For C specifically, verification is challenging because the language permits low-level memory manipulation, undefined behavior, and has complex semantics, making tools like separation logic-based verifiers \(e.g., the recently announced QCP\) and other heavyweight frameworks necessary. The proposal to unify programming and verification seeks to reduce the friction of writing annotated code separately from the proof effort.

## [Researcher factors RSA keys from a 1990s Certificate Authority](https://mcpherrin.ca/2026/09/07/rsa.html) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Security researcher Matt McPherrin demonstrated that RSA keys used by a 1990s-era Certificate Authority can be factored using modern hardware and algorithms. The achievement illustrates that cryptographic keys considered secure decades ago are now within reach of practical attacks. This demonstration highlights how legacy cryptographic material becomes vulnerable over time as computing power advances, underscoring the importance of key rotation and migration to longer key lengths. Organizations still relying on older RSA keys \(especially those under 2048 bits\) should reassess their security posture against modern factoring capabilities. The research likely targeted short-key RSA \(such as 512-bit\), which is well within the range of modern factoring efforts — for reference, 512-bit RSA has been considered practically breakable since 1999. The cost of factoring scales exponentially with key length, so each doubling of key bits dramatically increases difficulty, meaning 1024-bit and especially 2048-bit keys remain safe for now but should be monitored.

rss · Lobsters \(技术社区\) · Sep 8, 04:05

**Background**: RSA is a widely-used public-key cryptosystem based on the mathematical difficulty of factoring large composite numbers into their prime factors, invented by Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman in 1977. The security of RSA depends directly on key length: shorter keys are exponentially easier to factor, and advances in both algorithms \(like the General Number Field Sieve\) and hardware have steadily eroded the security of smaller keys. Certificate Authorities \(CAs\) issue digital certificates that underpin HTTPS and other PKI-based trust systems, so their key strength is critical to overall internet security.
