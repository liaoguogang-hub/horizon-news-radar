Germany&\#x27;s Federal Cartel Office \(Bundeskartellamt\) determined that Apple&\#x27;s App Tracking Transparency \(ATT\) framework applied stricter tracking permission prompts to third-party apps than to Apple&\#x27;s own apps, creating an unfair competitive advantage. Apple has agreed to equalize these prompts so that all apps—including its first-party ones—use the same tracking consent dialogs. This ruling reinforces the principle that privacy features cannot be used as a vehicle for anti-competitive self-preferencing, setting a precedent for how platform-level privacy controls are scrutinized in the EU and beyond. It affects millions of iOS users in the EU who will see consistent consent experiences across all apps, and signals to other gatekeeper platforms that differential treatment between first-party and third-party apps will attract regulatory intervention. Under the original ATT framework, third-party apps needed to display a prominent tracking permission prompt via Apple&\#x27;s ATT API, while Apple&\#x27;s own apps could engage in first-party tracking without such a prompt, relying only on disclosures in the App Store privacy label. Apple will now align its own apps&\#x27; prompts to match the same standard, though the exact scope—whether EU-only or global—remains to be clarified.

The framework was widely seen as a privacy-positive move that significantly impacted the digital advertising industry, particularly Meta, whose ad revenue dropped notably after ATT&\#x27;s launch.
